# **Footpaths and Fences: A Guide to Mosaic and Column Rights**

## **1\. The Harmony of the Duet: An Introduction**

In the tropical forests of Africa, the Barbet bird performs a remarkable feat: two birds sing in a perfect duet, their notes interlocking so precisely that they sound like a single voice. This is the inspiration for the **Barbets Duet**, a 20-year movement and experiment dedicated to harmonizing two seemingly contradictory worlds—African and Western knowledge. Our goal is to compose a new economic "song," one that replaces the rhythm of extraction with the melody of restoration.

The Barbets Duet seeks to heal a fundamental fracture in how we value our planet. In our current global economy, we treat the Earth like a warehouse of parts rather than a living system. This failure is captured perfectly in the **Oak Tree Paradox**:

"A mature oak tree can support 284 species of insect, provide food and nests for birds, acorns for mice and squirrels, and habitat for fungi. Its ‘life’ value is very high; its financial value is only realized once it is dead. Today’s environmental crisis is the consequence of this paradox."

To resolve this paradox, we must look at the "institutional rules" of the land. We are moving from a system that rewards the "death" of an oak tree to one that rewards the "abundance of life" it sustains. To do this, we must deconstruct the legal tools—the rights—that determine how we relate to the soil beneath our feet.

## **2\. The 'Column' Rights Model: The Way of the Fence**

The modern Western approach to land, which reaches its most absolute form in the United States, is the **Column Rights** model. It is a system built on the philosophy of **exclusivity**.

Imagine a square of land. In the Column model, you do not just own the surface; you own a vertical "column" that stretches from the core of the Earth to the edge of the atmosphere. This model creates a single, exclusive owner who has the absolute power to exclude all others. Its primary physical tool is the **fence**—a barrier that signals the vertical capture of wealth for one person alone.

A "column" of rights typically includes:

* **Air Rights:** Control over the sky and space above the land.  
* **Land & Buildings:** Exclusive use of the surface and any structures.  
* **Mineral Rights:** Ownership of the wealth—oil, gold, or water—buried deep underground.

While this model has been the engine of successful global markets, it creates an "Aha\!" moment of realization for the ecologist: when one person owns the "column," they are incentivized to extract its parts for profit rather than maintain the shared health of the whole.

## **3\. The 'Mosaic' Rights Model: The Way of the Footpath**

There is another way—a rediscovered wisdom often found in African traditions known as **Mosaic Rights**. This is an **inclusive** system. Here, the concept of "owning" the land is replaced by the concept of **Jumuiya**—a Swahili term describing a community with a shared vision, values, and goals.

In a mosaic landscape, no one person owns the "column." Instead, the land is seen as a rich tapestry of overlapping benefits. People do not build fences; they forge **footpaths**. These paths represent the literal flow of people accessing different "pieces" of the mosaic at different times. Because no one can exclude others from the land's health, everyone has a vested interest in maintaining high biodiversity.

### **Rights in a Mosaic Landscape**

| Group/Individual | Specific Right/Benefit Provided |
| :---- | :---- |
| **Women** | Rights to cultivate and harvest food crops for the family. |
| **Men** | Rights to manage and profit from tree crops (timber and fruit). |
| **Herders** | Rights to graze livestock on the fields after the harvest is complete. |
| **Well-Diggers** | Rights to the water from the well they manually dug. |
| **Government** | Specific, limited rights to deep underground minerals. |

By treating land as an inclusive mosaic, social equity and ecological health become inseparable.

## **4\. Side-by-Side: Fences vs. Footpaths**

To navigate the transition to a regenerative economy, we must compare the outcomes of these two legal theories. The Barbets Duet is not just a social project; it is a **business idea** intended to make the "Footpath" model as economically viable as the "Fence" model.

* **System: Column Rights**  
  * **Primary Tool:** The Fence (Exclusion)  
  * **Success Metric:** Market efficiency through resource extraction.  
* **System: Mosaic Rights**  
  * **Primary Tool:** The Footpath (Inclusion/Jumuiya)  
  * **Success Metric:** High biodiversity and social equity as a business asset.

This shift in theory is currently being tested on the ground at various "Learning Sites," where we are inventing the environmental markets of the future.

## **5\. The Barbets Duet in Action: 20 Years of Experimentation**

Our movement is grounded in "Learning Sites"—places where people live and work while testing the principles of the Duet. We are currently halfway through a 20-year experiment to prove that restoration is the ultimate driver of livelihood.

1. **Molo, Uganda:** Situated in the **Mt Elgon watershed**, James Magode Ikuya works with twelve families along the Kanginima stream. They are restoring a badly deforested landscape by introducing high-value "environmental" businesses like fish farming and beekeeping.  
   * *Lesson Learned:* Ecological restoration does not contradict economic activity; it is the foundation of new, resilient wealth.  
2. **Lukenya, Kenya:** On a rocky hillside in the **dry rangeland** outside Nairobi, Sammy Muvelah has established a tree nursery and moringa plantation. This site provides food, fuel, and even excursions for urban residents interested in the environment.  
   * *Lesson Learned:* By "just beginning" with a nursery, you create a tangible asset that provides ecological benefits even before the first harvest.  
3. **Woodland Valley Farm, UK:** Chris and Janet Jones run this organic, low-carbon farm in Cornwall. By producing beef and pork while acting as an outdoor classroom, they show that farming can be a tool for education rather than just extraction.  
   * *Lesson Learned:* Modern agriculture can adopt the "Jumuiya" spirit by opening the land to students and researchers as a shared resource.

These sites prove that we can move from theory to a lived reality where the "abundance of life" is the ultimate currency.

## **6\. Summary for the Aspiring Learner**

The path to a resilient future requires us to stop seeing land as a commodity to be fenced and start seeing it as a mosaic of life to be shared. Protecting our ecosystems is not a matter of charity; it is about **inventing new markets** that reward those who maintain the health of the Earth. As you look at your own landscape, remember the "Just Begin" principle: you don't need a grand plan to start a revolution; you only need to plant the first tree.

### **The 7 Principles of a Barbet Learning Site**

* \[ \] **Consistency with Site:** Let the specific ecology of the land (the "Mt Elgon" or "Dry Rangeland") dictate the activity.  
* \[ \] **Learning by Demonstration:** Show, don't just tell. Practical examples are the best teachers.  
* \[ \] **Just Begin:** Start with something enticing that bears fruit quickly to build momentum.  
* \[ \] **Thinking Step-by-Step:** Respect the natural order; every ecological process builds on the last.  
* \[ \] **Immediate Needs:** Ensure environmental goals support food security and income.  
* \[ \] **Path of Least Resistance:** Start with what is possible now, navigating the power structures that exist.  
* \[ \] **Utu Net Benefits:** Seek "Utu" (humanity)—ensure benefits flow to the individual, the community, and the environment simultaneously.
